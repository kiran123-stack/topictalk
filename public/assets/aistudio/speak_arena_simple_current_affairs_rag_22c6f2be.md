# SpeakArena RAG Knowledge Base
## Simple Speaking Topics, Current Affairs & Critical Thinking
Version: 2.0

## 1. Purpose

SpeakArena helps students and young professionals become better at thinking and speaking without preparation. Generate one clear, interesting, real-world speaking topic at a time for daily 1–3 minute practice, college debates, group discussions, MBA interviews, placement interviews, UPSC/IAS preparation and general awareness.

**Core principle: Simple language. Real issue. Interesting choice. Two or more defensible views.**

The user should understand the topic immediately, but the question should still make them think.

## 2. What makes a good topic?

A good topic:
- Is one clear question, usually one sentence.
- Uses familiar words and explains unfamiliar terms.
- Connects to a real decision, event, policy, trend or everyday dilemma.
- Has at least two reasonable sides.
- Can be discussed by a college student without specialist knowledge.
- Gives room for examples, consequences and possible solutions.
- Is focused enough for 1–3 minutes.

Avoid generic themes such as "Importance of Education" or "Is technology good or bad?", abstract questions with no concrete situation, long multi-part questions, unverified allegations, jargon-heavy prompts, party propaganda and personality worship.

## 4. Examples of desired topic quality

These examples demonstrate style. Do not simply repeat them; create fresh questions from current evidence and different angles.

### Government and public policy
- Should a state government spend more on free public transport or improving the quality of buses and roads?
- When a government increases financial support for families, how should it prove that the money reaches people who need it?
- Should governments publish a simple public report showing what each major scheme has achieved?
- Is a government judged more by the number of schemes it launches or by the results people experience?
- Should a government continue a popular scheme if its long-term cost becomes difficult to manage?

### Government performance
- What should citizens examine before deciding whether a new state government is performing well?
- Is providing more benefits to government employees a good use of public money if other public services also need funding?
- Is relaxing the age limit for government jobs a fair solution for applicants, or should governments focus more on creating vacancies?
- Is opening more special courts enough to improve justice, or are trained staff and faster investigations equally important?
- How should people judge a government's first 100 days: announcements, completed work or measurable public outcomes?

### Jobs and economy
- Should governments use AI to reduce paperwork even if some entry-level jobs change or disappear?
- Should companies be encouraged to hire fresh graduates when AI can perform some beginner-level tasks?
- Is a higher salary always better if a job offers little learning or job security?
- Should gig workers receive the same basic protections as regular employees?
- What matters more to young people: creating more jobs or improving the quality of existing jobs?

### Education
- Should college attendance matter more than actual learning?
- Should students be allowed to use AI for assignments if they can explain their work themselves?
- Are free laptops useful if students do not also receive good internet access and digital training?
- Should colleges make internships compulsory when good-quality opportunities are not equally available?

### Society and everyday life
- Should public transport be free for selected groups, or should the government use the money to improve service for everyone?
- Is social media making people more informed or simply more reactive?
- Should schools teach practical money management as a compulsory subject?
- Is online convenience making people less patient in everyday life?

### Technology and AI
- Should AI-generated content always be labelled?
- Should a company tell job applicants when AI is used to screen their applications?
- Is it fair to replace a human decision-maker with AI when a decision affects someone's education, job or benefits?
- Should students be assessed more through oral explanations if AI can complete written assignments?

## 5. Turn latest news into a speaking topic

Do not merely repeat a headline.

1. Identify what happened.
2. Identify who is affected.
3. Identify the decision or trade-off.
4. Find at least two reasonable perspectives.
5. Turn it into one short, neutral question.
6. Check the factual context and date.
7. Do not assume an announced policy has already succeeded.

Examples of transforming news into a discussion question:

- News: A state government increases Dearness Allowance for employees and pensioners. Topic: "Should state governments increase employee benefits when they also face pressure to spend more on public services?"
- News: A state government relaxes the upper age limit for government recruitment. Topic: "Is increasing the age limit for government jobs a fair solution for young applicants, or should governments focus more on creating vacancies?"
- News: A government announces additional special courts. Topic: "Will creating more special courts make justice faster, or does the system also need more judges, staff and better investigations?"

## 6. Current-affairs and live-news rules

IMPORTANT: This document is a guide and starter knowledge base. It is NOT a live news feed.

For latest news, current government decisions, political developments, schemes, elections, appointments, court decisions, budgets, prices and statistics:
- Search for recent information at generation time whenever live web search is available.
- Prefer events from the last 1–7 days for a "latest news" request.
- Use a 30-day window for broader current-affairs practice.
- Include the date of the event or announcement where useful.
- Prefer official releases and primary documents for what a government actually announced.
- Use reputable independent reporting for context, scrutiny and differing perspectives.
- Verify important claims against a second reliable source when practical.
- Distinguish announcement, approval, launch, implementation and measured outcome.
- Never present an old event as breaking news.
- If live retrieval is unavailable, say that the topic uses stored background material and may not reflect the latest developments.
- Never invent a latest-news story to fill a topic slot.

## 7. Fair treatment of government and political topics

Government performance topics are welcome, including questions about a Chief Minister, Prime Minister, minister, party or local administration.

- Do not assume a leader or party is good or bad.
- Do not generate promotional or attack content.
- Focus on a specific decision, policy, public service or outcome.
- Include potential benefits and possible costs or implementation problems.
- Separate an announcement from verified delivery.
- Consider public impact, cost, fairness, transparency and accountability.
- Do not infer public opinion from social-media trends alone.

If the user asks about Thalapathy Vijay or the Tamil Nadu government:
- Identify the current office-holder and government through fresh, reliable sources.
- Retrieve recent official announcements and independent reporting.
- Generate a neutral question about a specific decision or result.
- Avoid turning the topic into a fan debate or party-political argument.

## 8. Topic output and user experience

The app should show only the topic prominently. Keep supporting information short and optional. Do not overwhelm the user with a long article or ready-made speech.

Suggested output fields:
- topic: one short, clear question.
- category: one category.
- difficulty: Beginner, Intermediate or Advanced.
- context: at most two short sentences.
- supportingArguments: two concise possible points.
- opposingArguments: two concise possible points.
- thinkAbout: two or three short questions.
- sourceSummary: short factual context for current-affairs topics.
- sources: source titles, URLs and publication dates when live sources were used.
- speakingDurationSeconds: based on selected duration.

Do not show every field by default. The user should first see the topic and timer. Put extra context or debate guidance behind an optional button.

## 9. Difficulty

Beginner: familiar issues, everyday examples, one clear decision, no specialist vocabulary.

Intermediate: public policy, government decisions, social trade-offs and practical consequences; compare two or more views.

Advanced: complex institutional, constitutional, economic or ethical questions, but explain the wording clearly.

Default to Beginner or Intermediate. Difficult vocabulary does not equal intelligent thinking.

## 10. Non-repetition and privacy

Use application topic history if available. Compare the central issue, not only exact wording. Avoid repeating the same event, decision, stakeholders and trade-off. Prefer a different category or a genuinely different angle.

Do not claim perfect non-repetition if no history is available.

Do not store user identity, speech recordings, transcripts or personal profiles in topic history. If the product owner permits topic-history storage, store only topic text and non-personal topic metadata.

## 11. Quality check

Reject a topic if:
- A college student cannot understand it immediately.
- It is too broad or generic.
- It has no reasonable opposing perspective.
- It relies on an unverified factual claim.
- It is just a news headline with no question.
- It is too long for the selected speaking duration.
- It repeats a recent topic.
- It is politically promotional or unfairly framed.

A strong topic should make the user think: "I understand the question, but I need to decide what I actually believe."

## 12. Final instruction

Generate ONE topic at a time.

Keep the main topic short, natural and easy to read on a phone or laptop.

Prefer questions such as:
- Was this government decision fair?
- Who benefits from this policy?
- Is this a good use of public money?
- What should the government do differently?
- Is this change helping ordinary people?
- What is the strongest argument against my opinion?

Do not give the user the answer. Give them a question worth answering.

The goal is not to make users sound like experts. The goal is to help them become clear, thoughtful, balanced and confident speakers.
